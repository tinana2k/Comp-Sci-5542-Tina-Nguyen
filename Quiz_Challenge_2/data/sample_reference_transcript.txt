Good morning everyone, and welcome back to CS 5542. Today we are diving into one of the most
exciting and rapidly evolving areas of artificial intelligence — foundation models and their
practical applications in retrieval-augmented generation systems.

Let's begin with a quick recap. Last week we covered the transformer architecture introduced
by Vaswani et al. in 2017. The core insight was replacing recurrence with self-attention,
allowing the model to capture long-range dependencies in a single forward pass. This was the
architectural breakthrough that made large-scale pretraining feasible.

Now, building on that foundation, today we'll focus on three interconnected topics. First,
the landscape of modern foundation models. Second, how retrieval-augmented generation, or
RAG, addresses the knowledge limitation problem in large language models. And third, how we
evaluate these systems rigorously.

On the topic of foundation models: GPT-4, Llama 3, Claude 3, and Gemini Ultra represent the
current frontier. These models are pretrained on trillions of tokens and fine-tuned using a
technique called Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, or RLHF. This alignment step
is critical — it teaches the model to be helpful, harmless, and honest. However, I want you
to critically examine the claim that RLHF fully solves alignment. It does not. Hallucination
remains a persistent problem, as does sycophancy — where the model tells you what you want
to hear rather than what is true.

This brings us directly to RAG. Retrieval-Augmented Generation was introduced by Lewis et al.
in 2020 and has since become the dominant paradigm for grounding LLM responses in external,
verifiable knowledge. The architecture is straightforward: a dense retriever encodes both the
query and the document corpus into a shared embedding space using a bi-encoder like DPR or
ColBERT. At inference time, the top-k most relevant passages are retrieved and concatenated
with the user query before being passed to the generator LLM. This simple change dramatically
reduces hallucination rates and allows the model's knowledge to be updated without retraining.

For our lab this week, we will be building a RAG pipeline using LangChain and ChromaDB as the
vector store. Action item number one: please clone the lab repository and install the
dependencies listed in the requirements.txt file before Thursday's session. Action item
number two: read the original RAG paper by Lewis et al. — it is linked in the course portal.
Action item number three: each team must submit a one-paragraph project proposal by Friday at
midnight describing your chosen document corpus and retrieval strategy.

Now let's talk about evaluation. Evaluating RAG systems requires a multi-faceted approach.
For retrieval quality, we use Recall at K, Mean Reciprocal Rank, and NDCG. For generation
quality, we use ROUGE, BLEU, BERTScore, and increasingly, LLM-as-judge frameworks like RAGAS.
I want to emphasize: do not rely on a single metric. Each metric captures a different
dimension of quality, and gaming one often degrades another.

To summarize today's lecture: foundation models are powerful but imperfect, RAG is the
pragmatic solution to knowledge grounding, and evaluation must be multi-dimensional.
